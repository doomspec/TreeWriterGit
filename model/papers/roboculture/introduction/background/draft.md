Cell culture remains a foundational but labor-intensive component of experimental biology, requiring repetitive manipulations, stringent environmental control, and sustained operator attention over timescales that often extend beyond standard working hours. These characteristics make it an especially compelling target for laboratory automation, yet existing solutions occupy a fragmented design space. Self-driving laboratories have demonstrated the value of integrating robotics, computation, and adaptive experimentation to increase throughput and enable more systematic exploration, but most implementations have been optimized for discrete assay workflows rather than the continuous, context-dependent demands of living cell systems. Commercial liquid-handling platforms provide reliable execution of standardized pipetting routines, although they typically offer limited support for integrated sensing, decision-making, and recovery from perturbations during prolonged experiments. At the other extreme, highly customized industrial automation systems can deliver robust performance at scale, but they are expensive to deploy, difficult to reconfigure, and often too rigid for rapidly evolving research environments. As a result, a critical gap remains between flexible benchtop automation and fully engineered production systems: no broadly accessible platform currently unifies experimental flexibility, closed-loop feedback, and long-horizon autonomous operation for cell culture.
