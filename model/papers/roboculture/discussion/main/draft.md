Taken together, these results indicate that RoboCulture addresses a central bottleneck in laboratory automation: achieving reliable, high-precision manipulation in research environments whose physical layout and experimental needs change frequently. The visual-servoing strategy used for well access is particularly consequential in this regard, because it replaces plate-specific manual calibration with image-guided correction at the point of action, enabling accurate navigation to individual wells even when the workspace has been rearranged. The successful 15 h autonomous yeast-culture experiment further shows that this capability is not an isolated technical demonstration but can be sustained across an extended, multi-step biological workflow, integrating liquid handling, sensing, and decision logic without operator intervention. In contrast to many manipulator-based laboratory systems that attain robustness primarily through rigid environmental standardization, RoboCulture combines coarse modular localization, fine visual alignment, and behavior-tree control to preserve flexibility while maintaining operational reliability, an architectural choice that is well matched to the adaptive and iterative character of self-driving laboratory research @colledanchisebtrees @tom2024self. As a direct evolution of the open Digital Pipette platform, it also extends proven automated liquid-handling principles into a broader culture-management framework, thereby offering a practical and research-ready route toward autonomous experimentation in small-scale biological studies @digital-pipette.
