Developing a complete biofabrication workflow requires expertise that spans biology, mechanical engineering, microfabrication, robotics, software development, and data science [@editorial_pushing_2025; @huang_organoid_2025]. Such expertise rarely sits within a single laboratory. Individual technologies contribute different capabilities to tissue engineering. Complementary biofabrication techniques (e.g. bioprintin, enables the precise spatial organization of cells and biomaterials, microfabrication and microfluidics create controlled tissue microenvironments and integrated sensing, whereas automated liquid handling improves the reproducibility and scalability of tissue manufacturing [@huang_organoid_2025; @zhao_integrating_2024; @dettinger_open-source_2022]. Together, these technologies enable increasingly sophisticated tissue models. However, integrating them into a functional biofabrication workflow typically requires extensive customization, multidisciplinary expertise, and a detailed understanding of how independently developed technologies interact [@zhao_integrating_2024]. Successful implementation often requires detailed knowledge that extends beyond published methods, including proprietary hardware and software, laboratory-specific protocols, custom engineering solutions, and the tacit experience of individual researchers [@polanyi_tacit_1966]. Consequently, access to advanced biofabrication is determined not only by the availability of individual technologies but also by the ability to integrate and adapt them into cohesive experimental platforms. Overcoming these barriers will require more than incremental advances in individual technologies. It will require approaches that democratize both the tools and the knowledge needed to build interoperable biofabrication ecosystems [@maia_chagas_haves_2018].
