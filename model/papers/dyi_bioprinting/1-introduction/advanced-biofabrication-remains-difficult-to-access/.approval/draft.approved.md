Developing a complete biofabrication workflow requires expertise that spans biology, mechanical engineering, microfabrication, robotics, software development, automation, and data science [@editorial_pushing_2025; @huang_organoid_2025; @lo_review_2024]. Such expertise rarely sits within a single laboratory. Individual technologies contribute different capabilities to tissue engineering, but advanced biofabrication depends on their convergence into coordinated experimental systems rather than on any single instrument or method alone [@editorial_pushing_2025; @huang_organoid_2025]. Bioprinting provides architectural control over cells and biomaterials, including increasingly precise spatial patterning enabled by modern lithographic approaches [@dhand_lithography-based_2025]. Microfabrication and microfluidics enable physiologically relevant culture environments and integrated tissue interfaces, whereas automated liquid handling supports reproducible and scalable tissue manufacturing [@huang_organoid_2025; @zhao_integrating_2024; @dettinger_open-source_2022]. Together, these technologies enable increasingly sophisticated tissue models. However, integrating them into a functional biofabrication workflow typically requires extensive customization, multidisciplinary expertise, and a detailed understanding of how independently developed technologies interact [@huang_organoid_2025; @zhao_integrating_2024]. More broadly, experience from self-driving laboratories shows that successful experimental platforms also depend on the integration of hardware, software, automation, and human expertise rather than on isolated tools considered separately [@lo_review_2024]. Successful implementation therefore often requires knowledge that extends beyond published methods, including laboratory-specific protocols, custom engineering solutions, workflow orchestration, and the tacit experience of individual researchers [@polanyi_tacit_1966]. Consequently, access to advanced biofabrication is determined not only by the availability of individual technologies but also by the ability to integrate and adapt them into cohesive experimental platforms. Overcoming these barriers will require more than incremental advances in individual technologies. It will require approaches that democratize both the tools and the knowledge needed to build interoperable biofabrication ecosystems [@maia_chagas_haves_2018].
